RF FUNDAMENTALS
================
Radio frequency basics for SDR and wireless security research.


WHAT IS RF
----------
Radio frequency (RF) is electromagnetic energy in the range of about
3 kHz to 300 GHz. It travels at the speed of light and does not need
a physical medium — it propagates through air, walls, and vacuum. [1]

The relationship between frequency and wavelength:

    wavelength (m) = speed of light / frequency
    wavelength = 300,000,000 / frequency_in_hz

    908.42 MHz (Z-Wave)  ->  wavelength = 0.33 m  (about 13 inches)
    2.4 GHz (WiFi/BLE)   ->  wavelength = 0.125 m (about 5 inches)
    5.8 GHz (WiFi 5)     ->  wavelength = 0.052 m (about 2 inches)

Higher frequency = shorter wavelength = shorter range but more data
Lower frequency = longer wavelength = longer range but less data

This is why Z-Wave at 908 MHz reaches further through walls than
WiFi at 2.4 GHz, and why 5 GHz WiFi has shorter range than 2.4 GHz. [1]


FREQUENCY BANDS
---------------
Wireless protocols are assigned to specific frequency bands:

    Band              Frequency        Protocols
    ----              ---------        ---------
    Sub-GHz           300-928 MHz      Z-Wave, LoRa, 433 MHz OOK devices,
                                       garage openers, car key fobs
    ISM 2.4 GHz       2400-2483 MHz    WiFi, BLE, Zigbee, Thread, Matter
    ISM 5 GHz         5150-5850 MHz    WiFi 5/6
    ISM 6 GHz         5925-7125 MHz    WiFi 6E/7

"ISM" = Industrial, Scientific, Medical — unlicensed bands anyone can
use without a radio license, which is why IoT devices live here. [1]

The sub-GHz band is less standardized — different devices use different
frequencies and modulations, making it a fertile ground for finding
devices with weak or no security.


SIGNAL PROPERTIES
-----------------
Every RF signal has four properties:

    Frequency   — how many cycles per second (Hz)
    Amplitude   — how strong the signal is (power level)
    Phase       — where in its cycle the wave is at a given moment
    Bandwidth   — how wide the signal is in frequency space

Modulation changes one or more of these properties to encode data
onto the carrier wave. [1][2]


MODULATION — DIGITAL
---------------------
Digital modulation encodes discrete bits (1s and 0s) onto a carrier.
There are three fundamental types, corresponding to the three signal
properties you can change: [2][3]

ASK (Amplitude Shift Keying):
    - Amplitude changes to represent bits
    - Simplest to implement
    - Weakest noise immunity — noise corrupts amplitude easily

    OOK (On-Off Keying) is a special case of ASK:
        - Carrier ON = 1, carrier OFF = 0
        - The simplest possible digital modulation
        - Used by: 433 MHz garage openers, car key fobs, cheap RF
          remotes, weather stations
        - Easy to capture and replay — many devices have NO security
          beyond the modulation itself (CWE-294: replay attacks) [2][3]

FSK (Frequency Shift Keying):
    - Frequency shifts between two (or more) values to represent bits
    - Better noise immunity than ASK
    - Requires more bandwidth than ASK or PSK
    - GFSK (Gaussian FSK) applies a Gaussian filter to smooth
      transitions, reducing bandwidth — used by BLE and Z-Wave

    Z-Wave specifically uses NRZ FSK at 40 kbps:
        - Two frequencies: center +20 kHz = 0, center -20 kHz = 1
        - NRZ (Non-Return-to-Zero): the signal stays at the shifted
          frequency for the full bit period [2][3]

PSK (Phase Shift Keying):
    - Phase angle shifts to represent bits
    - BPSK: 0° = 0, 180° = 1 (1 bit per symbol)
    - QPSK: 4 phase positions (2 bits per symbol)
    - 8-PSK: 8 positions (3 bits per symbol)
    - Higher order = more bits per symbol but requires higher SNR
    - Used by: satellite communication, some WiFi modes [2][3]

QAM (Quadrature Amplitude Modulation):
    - Combines amplitude AND phase shifts
    - 16-QAM: 4 amplitude levels x 4 phase positions = 4 bits/symbol
    - 64-QAM: 6 bits/symbol, 256-QAM: 8 bits/symbol
    - Higher density = higher throughput but needs cleaner signal
    - Used by: WiFi (802.11ac uses up to 256-QAM, 802.11ax up to
      1024-QAM), cable TV, LTE [2][3]


POWER AND dBm
-------------
RF power is measured in dBm (decibels relative to 1 milliwatt).

    dBm     Power          Example
    ---     -----          -------
    +30     1 W            WiFi router (typical max)
    +20     100 mW         Bluetooth Class 1 (100m range)
    +10     10 mW          Z-Wave transmitter
    +4      2.5 mW         BLE (typical)
    0       1 mW           Reference point
    -10     0.1 mW         Very weak transmitter
    -30     0.001 mW       Received signal across a room
    -70     —              Decent WiFi signal (usable)
    -85     —              Weak WiFi signal (marginal)
    -100    —              Near noise floor for most receivers

The dB scale is logarithmic:
    +3 dB  = double the power
    -3 dB  = half the power
    +10 dB = 10x the power
    +20 dB = 100x the power

So a +30 dBm signal is 1000x more powerful than a 0 dBm signal. [4]


SNR — SIGNAL TO NOISE RATIO
-----------------------------
SNR = signal power - noise floor (in dB)

    Signal: -60 dBm, Noise floor: -90 dBm
    SNR = -60 - (-90) = 30 dB  (excellent — easy to decode)

    Signal: -80 dBm, Noise floor: -85 dBm
    SNR = -80 - (-85) = 5 dB   (terrible — barely decodable)

Every receiver has a minimum SNR to decode a given modulation:
    OOK:  ~10 dB (simple, tolerant)
    FSK:  ~12 dB
    BPSK: ~10 dB
    QPSK: ~13 dB
    64-QAM: ~26 dB (complex, demanding)

This is why WiFi falls back to lower data rates at distance — as SNR
drops, the AP switches from 256-QAM to 64-QAM to 16-QAM to QPSK to
BPSK, trading throughput for reliability. [4]


SDR — SOFTWARE DEFINED RADIO
------------------------------
Traditional radios have fixed hardware for each modulation and
frequency. An SDR digitizes the raw RF signal at the antenna and
does all demodulation in software. This means one device can receive
(and often transmit) on any frequency and any modulation the software
supports. [5]

The signal chain:

    Antenna -> LNA -> Mixer -> ADC -> [Digital IQ samples] -> Software

    LNA:   Low Noise Amplifier — boosts weak signals before processing
    Mixer: Shifts the signal down to a lower frequency (baseband)
    ADC:   Analog-to-Digital Converter — samples the analog signal

The output is IQ (In-phase / Quadrature) data — pairs of values
representing the signal's amplitude and phase at each sample point.
This is what hackrf_transfer captures to a file. [5]

IQ samples are stored as interleaved int8 values:
    [I0, Q0, I1, Q1, I2, Q2, ...]

    File size = sample_rate * 2 * duration_seconds bytes
    (factor of 2 because each sample has an I and Q value)

HackRF One specs relevant to this framework:
    Frequency range:   1 MHz to 6 GHz
    Sample rate:       up to 20 Msps
    Resolution:        8-bit (int8 IQ)
    Bandwidth:         up to 20 MHz
    Half-duplex:       can transmit OR receive, not both at once [5]


PROPAGATION
-----------
RF signals weaken as they travel. The main factors: [1]

Free-space path loss: signal weakens with the square of distance.
    Doubling the distance = 6 dB loss (1/4 the power)

Absorption: walls, floors, and materials absorb RF energy.
    Material          Approximate loss per pass
    --------          -------------------------
    Drywall           3 dB
    Wood door         4 dB
    Concrete wall     10-15 dB
    Brick             8-12 dB
    Metal             20+ dB (near-total block)

Reflection: signals bounce off metal and concrete surfaces, causing
multipath — the receiver gets the same signal via multiple paths
at slightly different times. This can cause constructive interference
(stronger signal) or destructive interference (signal cancellation).

Diffraction: signals bend around edges of obstacles. Lower frequencies
diffract more, which is why sub-GHz signals "wrap around" corners
better than 2.4 GHz. [1]
